FlyRank ML Internship — Capstone Research Paper

Can Search Visibility Predict What's About to Decline?

Khadija Majeed Machine Learning Track Lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring September 2026
Abstract

This project asks whether historical search-visibility and click-performance signals, measured over a three-month window, can identify content likely to see a future decline in search clicks. Using the FlyRank internship warehouse, features were built from March–May 2026 activity and tested against actual June 2026 outcomes, keeping the future window fully separate from the training data. A logistic regression model was compared against a transparent visibility-only baseline on the same held-out test set using Precision@K, ROC-AUC, and Average Precision. The baseline outperformed the model at every tested K, suggesting that raw historical visibility alone was a stronger predictor of future decline than the engineered feature set. The resulting ranked queue is offered as a decision-support tool for content review — not as a claim about how Google's ranking systems work.

Question

The research question, and the decision it's meant to support.

Research question

Can historical search visibility and click-performance signals identify content that is likely to experience future search-performance decline?

Decision supported

The output is meant to help a content or SEO reviewer decide which pages to look at first for a possible refresh — not to trigger any change automatically. The model produces a ranked priority queue, and a human makes the final call.

2

Data

The analysis uses the pseudonymized FlyRank internship warehouse release, aggregated to the content level before modeling.

  • Historical window: March, April, and May 2026 — used to build every feature.
  • Future window: June 2026 — held back entirely and used only to check what actually happened.
  • Fields used: Google Search Console availability, impressions, and clicks.
  • Fields excluded: client names, domains, URLs, private queries, credentials, and any pre-computed decision or action fields, to avoid circular modeling.

After merging the historical and future windows, 187,595 unique content items had usable data in both periods. Roughly 83,571 items had no recorded clicks in the historical window, so a percent-change figure could not be calculated for them; since the decline label requires at least 5 historical clicks to qualify at all, these items default to a non-decline label rather than an undefined one.

3

Methodology

Label definition

A content item is labeled 1 (declining) when it had at least 5 historical clicks and its average daily clicks fell by 20% or more in June compared with the March–May average. Everything else is labeled 0. This is an operational proxy, not a ground-truth measure of "true" decline.

Features

Total and average daily impressions and clicks, days with impressions, days with clicks, historical click-through rate, and maximum daily impressions — all computed only from the March–May window.

Baseline

A transparent baseline ranks content purely by historical search visibility (log-scaled impressions). It uses no label information and no model — it's the reference point the model has to beat.

Model

Logistic regression, chosen because the target is binary and its output probability doubles directly as a ranking score. Features were median-imputed and standardized inside a single pipeline to avoid leakage between train and test folds.

Validation

An 80/20 stratified train/test split was held out from the historical-feature, future-outcome dataset. The model and the baseline were scored on the exact same test set. June's actual performance was used only to build the label — never as a model input.

Honest finding: the transparent baseline — ranking purely by historical visibility — beat the logistic regression model at every tested value of K (Precision@10: 0.80 vs. 0.70; Precision@100: 0.75 vs. 0.58). This is a real result, not a failed experiment: it suggests the highest-visibility pages were already the most likely to show a future click decline, plausibly a regression-to-the-mean effect where high-traffic pages simply have more room to fall. In this experiment, the engineered feature set did not add ranking value beyond a simple visibility heuristic. That's a useful finding on its own — it points toward testing features the current set doesn't capture, such as content age, freshness, or SERP volatility, in a future iteration.
5

Limitations

  • This analysis is observational and intended for decision support, not automated action.
  • A high opportunity score does not mean refreshing a page will cause its performance to improve.
  • The decline label is an operational proxy built from observed click changes — not a directly verified business outcome.
  • Only a single future window (June 2026) was tested, so performance across other future periods is unknown.
  • The dataset doesn't capture page content quality, SERP features, competitor moves, or editorial changes — all of which can drive real decline independent of the signals used here.
  • This work makes no claim about how Google's ranking systems actually operate.
6

Ranked recommendations

The output is a review queue, not a publishing decision. Six ways to use it.

  1. Start with high-visibility, high-score pages

    These carry the most observed search exposure, so getting them right matters most.

  2. Investigate before refreshing

    Look at the historical-to-June pattern for each flagged page before deciding a refresh is the right move.

  3. Separate CTR problems from content problems

    Strong visibility with weak click-through often points to a title or snippet fix, not a full rewrite.

  4. Treat a high score as a prompt to review, not to act

    Given the baseline's edge over the model, a high opportunity score should trigger a look, not an automatic edit.

  5. Leave strong, low-score performers alone

    Monitoring is usually more appropriate than unnecessary intervention.

  6. Keep a human in the loop

    This ranking prioritizes attention. The refresh, rewrite, or no-action call is an editorial decision.
